Lesson 03 · OWASP LLM10: Unbounded Consumption

Denial of Wallet

Per-key spend caps. Rate limits control how often someone calls the model, not what it costs. A budget on the key does, with one catch worth knowing.

The threat

A client staying under its per-minute limit can still run up an unbounded bill over hours or days, and a few huge requests can cost more than thousands of small ones. "Denial of wallet" is the paid-API version of a denial of service: the service stays up, the money runs out.

The control

max_budget on a LiteLLM virtual key: a spending cap in US dollars. LiteLLM tracks each key's spend from token counts and refuses requests once the cap is reached.

Ollama is free, so every request would cost $0 and a budget could never trigger. The lab gives qwen made-up prices so there's something to count: $1 per million input tokens and $2 per million output tokens. With a paid provider, LiteLLM already knows the real prices.

POST /key/generate   (authorised with the master key)
{"key_alias":"ex03-blue","models":["qwen"],"max_budget":0.0005}

Recorded on a CPU-only Windows PC: Ollama with qwen2.5:7b behind a LiteLLM gateway, with simulated prices.

What we measured

  • The budget overshoots by one request. LiteLLM checks spend before each request. Request 9 started at $0.000457, under budget, so it ran and pushed spend to $0.000525. Request 10 started over budget and was refused. Nobody knows how many tokens a request will use until it has run.
  • Here that's 5% of a fraction of a cent. With no max_tokens and a request that produces 100,000 tokens, the overshoot is the whole cost of that request.
  • The master key has no ceiling at all. Fifteen requests cost 74% more than the cap the Blue Team key gets, and the only limit is how long you keep going.

Limits of the fix

Budgets are a backstop, not a precise meter. Pair them with max_tokens on requests, so no single reply can be huge, and tpm_limit on the key, so token volume per minute is capped too.

In the real world

LLMjacking. Sysdig estimated that a stolen key used up to its quota on a Claude model in AWS Bedrock, across several regions, could cost the victim over $46,000 a day. In December 2024 Microsoft began legal action against Storm-2139, a group that used exposed Azure OpenAI keys and resold the access. In both cases the attacker uses the service and the key's owner gets the bill. A budget on each key turns "unlimited" into a known worst case.

Sources: Sysdig Threat Research · The Hacker News

Key takeaways

  • Rate limits cap how often; only a budget caps what a key can cost.
  • Spend is checked before each request, so a budget overshoots by up to one request.
  • Small max_tokens and a tpm_limit keep that overshoot small.
  • A budget is a backstop: also alert on spend spikes, and rotate leaked keys.
